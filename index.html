01 / Understand the basics

What is hypertension?

Your heart pushes blood through arteries. Blood pressure is the force of that blood against the artery walls.

It rises and falls with activity, stress, sleep, and other factors. When it stays higher than recommended over time, it is called hypertension. You can feel completely well and still have it, so measurement matters.

In the WHO guidance for adults, hypertension is generally diagnosed when properly measured blood pressure is 140/90 mmHg or higher on different days. Some countries use lower thresholds; your clinician applies the guideline relevant to you.

Typical “normal” range in many guidelines.

Above 180 and/or 120

Very high. Repeat after at least 1 minute and follow urgent guidance.

Thresholds and targets differ by guideline, age, pregnancy, and health conditions. The higher number in either position matters.

!

Get urgent help for warning symptoms. Chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden weakness or numbness, vision changes, or difficulty speaking can be emergencies. Contact local emergency services now. If a repeated reading is above 180/120 with any of these symptoms, do not wait for it to come down.

BRAIN & BLOOD VESSELS

A higher chance of stroke

Long-term high pressure can damage brain arteries. A vessel may become blocked or rupture, causing a stroke. Persistent hypertension is also associated with cognitive decline later in life.

04 / It is not one-size-fits-all

Where does high blood pressure come from?

↗

Primary hypertension

Most cases develop gradually through a mix of factors, including family history, age, diet, activity, sleep, and other health conditions.

⌕

Secondary hypertension

Sometimes another condition or medicine contributes. Kidney or hormone disorders and some medicines can be involved.

◎

Family history

High blood pressure in close relatives can increase your chance of developing it. It does not prove a single inherited syndrome or gene.

For younger adults

High readings at a young age deserve a proper assessment. A clinician may review measurement technique, medicines, family history, and whether further evaluation is appropriate.

Give your body a quiet moment.

Avoid caffeine, smoking, and exercise for 30 minutes beforehand. Empty your bladder, then sit quietly for at least five minutes.

06 / Everyday care

Small habits add up.
Care is personal.

Daily choices can support healthy blood pressure. Many people also need medicine. A care plan should fit your health, preferences, and circumstances.

01

Eat for heart health

Choose more vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains, and minimally processed foods. Ask about salt reduction that fits your diet.

02

Move regularly

Build activity in a way that feels safe and sustainable. If you have a health condition, ask your clinician what is right for you.

03

Take medicines as agreed

Do not start, stop, or change a blood pressure medicine on your own. Ask your prescriber or pharmacist about side effects or missed doses.

04

Keep a record

Bring your readings, medicines, and questions to appointments. A log can help your care team see patterns over time.

COMMON MYTH

“I feel fine, so my pressure must be fine.” High blood pressure often causes no symptoms. Measurement—not how you feel—is how you know.
